2Findings in brief#

  1. The core claim survives scrutiny, with sharper edges than the folk version. Evolved skills — plain-markdown procedural modules discovered from an agent’s own execution traces — lift task performance without touching weights across five models and five benchmarks (23 of 25 model-benchmark pairs improve) [P Tab. 1]. A 9B model with evolved skills beats a 27B model without them (47.4% vs 39.4% average) [P §4.2.1].
  2. But the marketing intuition “skills help small models most” is backwards. Absolute gains grow with model scale (+12.3 / +17.5 / +23.9 points for Qwen 4B/9B/27B) [P §4.2.1]. The correct economic claim is different and stronger: skills let a cheaper model do a bigger model’s job — and skills evolved by one model transfer to another, so the discovery cost can be paid once, by whichever model discovers best [P Tab. 2].
  3. Skill discovery and skill execution are distinct capabilities. Transferred skills frequently beat self-evolved ones (e.g. Qwen-9B on ALFWorld: 70.2% with 27B-evolved skills vs 63.4% with its own) [P §4.2.2]. There is also a capability floor: a model too weak to execute a multi-step procedure gains nothing from owning it, and over-specific skills can transfer negatively (50.5% → 18.1% in the worst case) [P §4.2.2].
  4. The single most important mechanism is the persistent wiki, not the skill format. Giving the skill-proposing agent a compounding knowledge base — rather than just raw traces — is worth +15.0 points on its own (48.7% → 63.7%, Gemini-3.5-Flash four-benchmark average) [P Tab. 3]. This is the paper’s direct validation of Karpathy’s LLM-Wiki thesis: knowledge should be compiled once and kept current, not re-derived per query [G].
  5. Separation of layers is the recurring design law. All three sources converge on the same decomposition — immutable raw experience, compiled knowledge, and executable procedure — with an audit trail and a validation gate between knowledge and procedure. The wiki is never rolled back; skills are [P §3.2.4] [G] [A].
  6. Counter-intuitive detail worth remembering: letting the executing agent read the wiki during training rollouts makes the final skills worse (63.7% → 60.9%) — knowledge leaking into execution robs the optimizer of signal [P §5.1]. Layer boundaries are load-bearing.
  7. The whole loop is cheap where it matters. WikiSkill’s optimizer overhead is constant per iteration (one maintainer call plus a 10–20-turn proposer agent), independent of training-set size; rollouts dominate cost [P App. D]. Training sets were tiny: 16–80 tasks [P Tab. 6].
  8. autoresearch shows the harness can be almost embarrassingly small. Three files, one metric, a keep/discard rule, and an overnight run: 83 experiments, 15 kept improvements, on a ~50M-parameter model with 5-minute training runs [A README, progress.png]. That — not another heavyweight project — is the right shape for this lab’s first validation of skill evolution (§8).

3Source ②: the LLM Wiki pattern — compile, don’t re-retrieve#

Karpathy’s “LLM Wiki” gist is a deliberately abstract idea file — “it describes the idea, not a specific implementation” — meant to be handed to a coding agent, which then instantiates the specifics collaboratively [G]. It is the conceptual seed the WikiSkill paper explicitly credits: “Inspired by Karpathy (2026)’s perspective on LLM Wiki, which advocates compiling experience into persistent, compounding knowledge” [P §1].

3.1 The core idea: accumulation beats retrieval#

The gist positions itself against the default RAG mental model, where “the LLM is rediscovering knowledge from scratch on every question. There’s no accumulation.” Instead:

“The LLM incrementally builds and maintains a persistent wiki — a structured, interlinked collection of markdown files that sits between you and the raw sources. […] The knowledge is compiled once and then kept current, not re-derived on every query. […] The wiki is a persistent, compounding artifact.”

— Karpathy, “LLM Wiki” [G]

The division of labor is explicit: the human curates sources, directs analysis, and asks questions; the LLM does all the maintenance — “the summarizing, cross-referencing, filing, and bookkeeping.” His working image: “Obsidian is the IDE; the LLM is the programmer; the wiki is the codebase.” The claimed reason the pattern works is economic, not technical: humans abandon wikis because “the maintenance burden grows faster than the value,” while for an LLM “the cost of maintenance is near zero” [G]. He traces the lineage to Vannevar Bush’s Memex (1945) — a curated personal knowledge store with associative trails — noting that “the part he couldn’t solve was who does the maintenance. The LLM handles that” [G][13].

3.2 Architecture and operations#

Three layers [G §Architecture]:

  • Raw sources — curated, immutable source documents. “The LLM reads from them but never modifies them. This is your source of truth.”
  • The wiki — LLM-generated markdown: summaries, entity pages, concept pages, comparisons, a synthesis. “You read it; the LLM writes it.”
  • The schema — a configuration document (his examples: CLAUDE.md for Claude Code, AGENTS.md for Codex) defining structure, conventions, and workflows. “It’s what makes the LLM a disciplined wiki maintainer rather than a generic chatbot. You and the LLM co-evolve this over time.”

Three operations run over these layers: Ingest (a single source may touch 10–15 wiki pages: summary page, index update, entity/concept page updates, log entry), Query (search index → read pages → synthesize with citations — and crucially, “good answers can be filed back into the wiki as new pages,” so explorations compound too), and Lint (periodic health checks for contradictions, stale claims, orphan pages, missing cross-references) [G §Operations].

Two special files carry navigation: index.md (content-oriented catalog — one line per page; the LLM reads it first when answering, which “works surprisingly well at moderate scale (~100 sources, ~hundreds of pages) and avoids the need for embedding-based RAG infrastructure”) and log.md (chronological, append-only, with grep-parseable entry prefixes) [G §Indexing]. Tooling stays optional and incremental — a markdown search engine like qmd only once the index file stops being enough[12].

Why this source matters here

Everything in the gist is qualitative — it makes no measured claims. Its significance for this lab is that WikiSkill (§4) turned exactly this architecture into a controlled experiment and measured the value of the middle layer: the compounding wiki is worth +15.0 points of task accuracy to a skill optimizer on its own (Gemini-3.5-Flash, four-benchmark average) [P Tab. 3]. The gist supplies the design language; the paper supplies the evidence.

4Source ①: WikiSkill — the empirical test of compiled experience#

WikiSkill: Compiling Agent Experience into Persistent Knowledge for Skill Evolution (Tang, Rashtchian, Ferng, Tomkins, Juan, Vu — Google Research, with a Virginia Tech affiliation; arXiv:2608.27454v1, 27 Aug 2026, CC BY 4.0)[1]. The paper sits in a fast-moving 2025–2026 literature on skill evolution: automatically discovering agent skills from execution experience rather than authoring them by hand. Its named competitors are EvoSkill (frontier search over candidate skill programs, failure traces + flat feedback history)[6], Trace2Skill (per-trajectory analysts whose lessons are hierarchically merged into patches)[7], and SkillOpt (a six-stage “ReflACT” pipeline maintaining one monolithic skill document)[8] [P §1, App. D.1].

Its diagnosis of all three: “these methods do not maintain what has been learned as a separate, evolving knowledge representation” — insights stay scattered across optimization histories [P §1, §6]. WikiSkill’s move is to insert a persistent, never-rolled-back knowledge layer between raw experience and executable skills.

What “skill” means here

A skill is “a modular, filesystem-based directory that packages domain-specific procedural knowledge into instructions, scripts, and other resources” — concretely a SKILL.md with frontmatter metadata (name, concise description), full procedural instructions, and applicability conditions [P §2]. This is the open format popularized by Anthropic’s Agent Skills work, which the paper cites directly[4][5]. WikiSkill adds one file per skill: PURPOSE.md, mapping the skill back to the wiki patterns that motivated it [P §3.1]. Important experimental control: evolved skills are injected in full into the inference agent’s system prompt — no retrieval or triggering step — so skill quality is measured without retrieval failures as a confound [P §3.2.1, Limitations].

4.1 Three-layer architecture#

The agent workspace is split into three directories [P §3.1] — a direct formalization of the gist’s raw / wiki / schema split:

  • raw/ — immutable execution traces from each iteration’s training rollouts: full step-by-step reasoning, tool calls, outputs, final answers.
  • wiki/ — the persistent knowledge base: patterns/ (one markdown page per failure mode or successful strategy, with root cause and workaround), index.md (one-line catalog per pattern: problem + root cause + fix), an evolution log, and skill-impact.md — an audit trail written programmatically by the harness, recording every proposal’s metadata, target skill, unified diff, validation score, and accept/reject outcome [P §3.1, §3.2.4]. The wiki is never reset and never rolled back.
  • skills/ — the active skill set: per skill, SKILL.md + PURPOSE.md. Subject to validation gating and rollback.

4.2 The evolution loop#

State at iteration k is the pair (Sₖ, Wₖ) — skill set and wiki — starting from (∅, ∅). Each of K iterations runs [P §2, §3.2, Alg. 1]:

  1. Rollout. The Inference Agent executes all training tasks with the current skills injected in full into its system prompt; traces land in raw/.
  2. Consolidate. The Wiki Maintainer receives the full current wiki plus a stratified sample of traces — at most 8 per iteration: up to 5 failures (root-cause analysis) and up to 3 successes (strategy extraction), each log capped at 15,000 characters [P App. C]. It creates/patches pattern pages, rewrites index.md, appends to the log. Pattern pages are told to be “10–30 lines, not essays” [P App. E.2].
  3. Propose. The Skill Proposer runs as a multi-turn ReAct agent[10] (≈10–20 turns in practice). It starts from the wiki index, skill-impact.md, and a pass/fail summary of all training tasks, then pulls specific pattern pages and raw traces on demand (it must read at least 4 traces before proposing [P App. E.3]). Output: one atomic proposal — create one skill, or patch one skill.
  4. Gate. The candidate skill set is scored on the validation split. Accepted only if it strictly beats the best validation score so far; otherwise skills roll back. Either way, the harness appends the diff, score, and outcome to skill-impact.md. Evolution stops early if validation hits 100% [P §3.2.4].

The rejected-proposal record is not bookkeeping trivia — the proposer prompt makes it operational: “Read wiki/skill-impact.md to see what was tried before (includes full content of rejected proposals — DO NOT repeat rejected approaches)” [P App. E.3]. That is the mechanism by which the system learns from its own optimization failures, not just from task failures.

4.3 Main results#

Setup: five benchmarks spanning single-step math (LiveMathematicianBench), agentic web search (SealQA), code-mediated spreadsheet manipulation (SpreadsheetBench), long-context document QA over Treasury bulletins (OfficeQA), and interactive embodied tasks (ALFWorld)[14]; five models — Qwen-3.5-4B/9B-Instruct, Qwen-3.6-27B, Gemma-4-31B-It (all via vLLM), and Gemini-3.5-Flash [P §4.1]. In each condition, the same model plays every role (inference, maintainer, proposer) — cross-model splits come later in §4.4. All scores average three independent runs of the full evolution; significance via paired bootstrap, 1,000 resamples, p < 0.05 [P §4.2, App. C].

Three headline findings

  • Consistency, not just peak performance. WikiSkill has the best average for all five models, beating the strongest competing method by +3.3, +5.1, +10.0, +5.8, and +12.0 points (4B, 9B, 27B, Gemma, Flash respectively). The baselines are erratic — EvoSkill lifts Qwen-9B on LiveMath (28.2→58.1) but degrades Gemma-31B on the same benchmark (33.9→29.8) [P §4.2.1].
  • Skill evolution complements scaling — gains grow with capability. Within the Qwen family: +12.3 (4B), +17.5 (9B), +23.9 (27B) average points over no-skill. Most dramatic on SpreadSheet: +6.5, +9.3, +40.9. Yet skills also compensate for scale: Qwen-9B+WikiSkill (47.4) beats Qwen-27B bare (39.4) [P §4.2.1].
  • Domain amenability varies a lot. For Qwen-27B: +40.9 (SpreadSheet), +28.0 (LiveMath), +24.8 (ALFWorld) vs +14.1 (SealQA), +11.6 (OfficeQA). Procedural, tool-mediated, error-recoverable domains benefit most; long-context navigation least — and on OfficeQA the 4B model actually degraded slightly (30.2→28.5) because it could not execute the multi-step search workflows its own skills prescribed [P §4.2.1].

Four findings from this table, all load-bearing for the lab’s interests [P §4.2.2]:

  1. Transfer works, and often beats self-evolution. Qwen-27B’s skills lift Qwen-9B to 50.5% on SpreadSheet (vs 24.3 bare, 33.6 self-evolved) and 70.2% on ALFWorld (vs 63.4 self-evolved). The paper’s conclusion, as its introduction phrases it [P §1]: “skill discovery and skill execution are distinct capabilities.”
  2. Transfer runs uphill too. The tiny 4B model’s skills improve Gemma-31B to 73.1% on LiveMath and even nudge Gemini Flash on ALFWorld (85.9→87.3). “Stronger source models do not necessarily produce better skills.”
  3. Negative transfer is real and mechanistically understood. 4B-evolved SpreadSheet skills crater Gemini Flash from 50.5% to 18.1%: the small model’s skills encode low-level workarounds (single-line Python commands, string-conversion rules) that handcuff a stronger model’s end-to-end scripting, and fragmented diagnostic steps exhaust its interaction budget. General procedures transfer; model-specific crutches don’t.
  4. Execution has a capability floor. The same 27B-evolved SpreadSheet skills yield +18.4 / +26.2 / +40.9 for 4B / 9B / 27B executors — identical knowledge, more than double the value as executor capability rises. And OfficeQA shows a skill can help another model more than its author: 4B’s skills hurt itself (30.2→28.5) but lift 27B by +10.8 (42.1→52.9).

Two conclusions, both structural rather than incremental:

  • The wiki is the engine. Same skill format, same gating, same proposer — adding persistent consolidated knowledge to the proposer’s inputs moves the average from 48.7% to 63.7% (+15.0). “Without persistent knowledge accumulated across iterations, the Skill Proposer struggles to resolve intricate failure modes” [P §5.1].
  • The executor must stay blind. Also granting the Inference Agent wiki access during training rollouts drops the result to 60.9% (LiveMath 72.6→64.8). The paper’s hypothesis: the agent solves tasks from the wiki instead of from skills, making trajectories less informative for skill development [P §5.1]. Knowledge for the optimizer and procedure for the executor are different products and must not leak into each other during training.

4.6 What evolved skills and wikis actually look like#

Concreteness worth keeping, because it calibrates expectations for any reimplementation [P §5.2, Tab. 4–5]:

  • Skills are short. Average evolved-skill length ranges from 45.1 markdown lines (Gemma-4-31B) to 128.6 (Qwen-9B). By benchmark: SpreadSheet longest (142.5 lines), LiveMath shortest (84.6). These are pages, not manuals.
  • Few skills survive gating. Per run, roughly 2.3–4.8 skill creations are proposed but only ≈1.2–1.6 accepted; edits face an even harsher gate (3.2–5.7 proposed, 0.7–1.3 accepted). The gate rejects most of what the proposer produces — that is the design, not a failure.
  • The wiki grows freely. 6.3–8.9 patterns created and 7.0–18.4 pattern edits on average (never gated, never rolled back), each pattern averaging 18–48 lines.
  • Refinement continues late. Only 39–52% of accepted updates by model (39–58% by benchmark) land in iterations 0–1; the rest arrive mid (2–4) and late (5–7) — on SealQA, 28% of accepted updates come in iterations 5–7. Skill evolution is not a one-shot distillation [P App. A.2, Tab. 5].

The paper’s case study (Qwen-3.6-27B on ALFWorld) shows the full causal chain [P §5.3]: iteration 0 — the maintainer files a pattern for a take-examine-move loop; the proposer’s goal-directed-action skill fails validation and is rejected, with its diff preserved. Iteration 1 — informed by that audit trail, the proposer instead creates break-repetition-loop with a concrete action rule (“Never Return an Item to Its Origin Location”), which is accepted. Iteration 4 — new loop variants appear in traces, the maintainer accumulates evidence (multi-operation-loop.md), and the skill is refined with a second rule (“Each Operation Type ONCE Per Item”). Compiled knowledge → targeted procedure → evidence-driven refinement, exactly the loop the architecture promises.

The wiki is what makes full-batch O(1) viable: instead of pushing every trace through the optimizer, the proposer navigates an index of compiled knowledge and pulls only the traces it needs. Compare training-set sizes — 16 (SealQA) to 80 (SpreadSheet) tasks, validation 10–40, test 85–280 [P Tab. 6]. Total cost is dominated by rollouts: K iterations × (Ntrain + Nval) trajectories, times three runs in the paper’s protocol.

4.8 Stated limitations#

  • No retrieval/triggering evaluated. Full prompt injection sidesteps the question of whether agents would choose to load the right skill — “which becomes important as the number of available skills grows” [P Limitations]. (This is the progressive-disclosure problem the skills literature already flags [P §1].)
  • Strict gating discards neutral stepping stones. A proposal that holds performance flat but enables later gains is rejected. Adopted for baseline comparability, flagged as future work.
  • No wiki pruning. The wiki compounds without garbage collection; the authors expect pruning to become necessary on longer runs.
  • No very-long-horizon tasks (hundreds of actions / multi-hour executions), and no within-episode skill adaptation.
  • Our additional note (not the paper’s): validation splits are small (10–40 tasks), which the authors acknowledge introduces gating noise, mitigated by averaging three full evolution runs [P App. B]. Any reimplementation inherits this noise — see the seed-change anecdote in §5.3 and the statistics conventions this lab already maintains (§7).

5Source ③: autoresearch — the minimal harness archetype#

karpathy/autoresearch (March 2026, MIT)[3] is the other half of the inspiration: not a knowledge system but a demonstration of how small a credible autonomous-improvement harness can be. The idea, from its README: “give an AI agent a small but real LLM training setup and let it experiment autonomously overnight. It modifies the code, trains for 5 minutes, checks if the result improved, keeps or discards, and repeats.”

5.1 Three files and a rule#

The training target is a simplified single-GPU nanochat[11]: a GPT at DEPTH = 8 (~50M parameters), 8,192-token BPE vocab, 2,048 context, Muon + AdamW, trained on a fixed web-data mix for a hard wall-clock budget of 300 seconds per run [A prepare.py, train.py, program.md]. The whole system is three files with strict ownership:

  • prepare.py — constants, data, tokenizer, dataloader, and the ground-truth metric (evaluate_bpb). Nobody modifies it. (The immutable layer.)
  • train.py — model, optimizer, training loop. The agent modifies only this. (The procedure layer.)
  • program.md — the agent’s standing instructions. The human modifies only this. The README names it exactly: “The program.md file is essentially a super lightweight ‘skill’.”

The metric is validation bits-per-byte (val_bpb) — chosen because it is vocabulary-independent, so architectural changes stay comparable. The fixed 5-minute budget makes every experiment comparable regardless of what the agent changed, and yields “approx 12 experiments/hour and approx 100 experiments while you sleep” [A README].

5.2 The loop, and where knowledge lives#

program.md specifies a WikiSkill-shaped loop in miniature: edit train.py → commit → run → grep val_bpb from the log → record a row in results.tsv (commit, val_bpb, memory_gb, status ∈ {keep, discard, crash}, description) → advance the branch if the metric improved, git reset if not [A program.md]. The gate is the same strict rule as WikiSkill’s (accept iff strictly better); git is the rollback mechanism; results.tsv plus the git log are the audit trail. It even carries an autonomy clause worth quoting for its bluntness: “Do NOT pause to ask the human if you should continue… If you run out of ideas, think harder — read papers referenced in the code, re-read the in-scope files for new angles, try combining previous near-misses.”

What autoresearch conspicuously lacks is WikiSkill’s middle layer: there is no pattern catalog, no consolidation step, no persistent “what we learned and why” — only one-line descriptions in a TSV and diffs in git history. That is precisely the configuration WikiSkill’s ablation measured at 48.7% vs 63.7% (§4.5): traces and outcome history without compiled knowledge. The two projects are therefore perfect complements: autoresearch shows the minimum viable harness; WikiSkill shows the highest-leverage missing organ for it.

5.3 The demo that proves the shape#

Two lessons ride on that last kept point. First, the harness works: an unattended agent produced 83 controlled experiments and a ≈2% val_bpb improvement overnight, most gains arriving as small, legible, individually-gated changes. Second, gating on a noisy metric will eventually accept noise: “random seed 42→137” improved validation bpb and was kept, but a seed is not an improvement — it is variance harvesting. WikiSkill hits the same issue from the other side (small validation splits, mitigated by averaging three full runs [P App. B]); this lab’s existing statistics doctrine (pre-registered thresholds, paired tests, refusal of unlicensed claims — §7) is exactly the antidote, and any MVP here should apply it (§8).

Scale-down guidance worth keeping: the README sketches how to shrink the rig for small machines (narrower TinyStories-style data, smaller vocab / byte-level tokenizer, shorter sequences, DEPTH 8→4, full-attention window pattern, smaller total batch)[15], and links community forks for macOS/MLX/Windows/AMD [A README]. The same shrink-everything-but-the-loop philosophy drives our MVP design in §8.

6Synthesis: one design pattern, three instantiations#

Read together, the three sources describe a single recurring architecture for improving an AI system without touching model weights: separate what happened (immutable), what we know (compounding), and what to do (gated); let agents compile the first into the second and propose the third; accept proposals only against a measured criterion; and keep an audit trail the optimizer itself can read. The differences are mostly about which parts are automated and how rigorously acceptance is tested.

6.2 The “fine-tuning without weights” thesis, examined#

The motivating intuition for this report was: well-tuned skills act like fine-tuning without touching weights — spend frontier-level tokens once to compile capability into small units, then run cheaper models with guidance instead of a frontier model figuring everything out each time. The evidence supports a sharpened version of each half:

What the evidence supports

  • Skills move performance the way training would, at text speed. +12 to +24 average points across model scales, up to +40.9 on a single benchmark, from markdown files of 45–143 lines [P Tab. 1, Tab. 4]. The paper frames skills explicitly as capturing expertise “without updating model parameters,” consistent with the Agent Skills format they build on[4].
  • The artifact is portable in a way weights never are. Skills evolved by one model transfer across scales and vendors (Qwen→Gemma, Qwen→Gemini, Gemini→Qwen), sometimes beating self-evolved skills [P Tab. 2]. A LoRA does not transfer across model families; a SKILL.md does.
  • “Small + compiled guidance ≥ large + nothing” is real. 9B+skills (47.4) > 27B bare (39.4); 4B+skills (38.5) sits just under 27B bare [P §4.2.1]. This is the substitution the economic argument needs.
  • And unlike fine-tuning, every unit is inspectable, reviewable, diffable, and rollbackable — properties this lab’s governance doctrine already demands of interventions. The audit trail (skill-impact.md) is a first-class part of the method, not an afterthought.

Where the folk version needs correcting

  • Gains grow with executor capability, not against it (+12.3 → +23.9 across Qwen 4B→27B) [P §4.2.1]. Skills are not a prosthetic that rescues weak models; they are a multiplier whose factor rises with the model’s ability to follow procedures. The cheapest adequate executor, not the cheapest executor, is the target.
  • There is a floor. Below the capability needed to execute a procedure, a skill is worthless or harmful (4B on OfficeQA: −1.7 with its own skill) [P §4.2.2].
  • Frontier-evolved is not automatically better. Discovery quality is not monotonic in model size (4B skills lifting 31B and Flash on some tasks; Flash skills losing to 27B skills on others) [P Tab. 2]. Whoever’s traces expose the failure modes best is the best teacher for that domain–executor pair.
  • Skills must encode general procedures, not the discoverer’s crutches — else negative transfer (50.5→18.1) [P §4.2.2]. If we evolve skills with a frontier model for a cheap executor, the evolution loop should roll out with the executor, exactly as WikiSkill separates proposer from inference agent.
  • Domain matters more than anything. Amenability ranged from ~+11 to ~+41 points for the same model [P §4.2.1]. Procedural, tool-mediated, verifiable domains are where this pays.

6.3 The economics: spend frontier tokens once, run cheap tokens forever#

The right cost model, assembled from the paper’s own accounting [P App. B–D]:

C_evolve ≈ K · [ N_train + N_val ] · c_rollout        (rollouts dominate)
         + K · [ 1 + T_ReAct ] · c_optimizer          (T_ReAct ≈ 10–20; O(1) per iteration)
         × R runs if averaging for gating robustness   (paper: R = 3)

with paper-scale numbers: K ≈ 8 (inferred from the paper's iteration 0–7 grouping, Tab. 5),
N_train = 16–80, N_val = 10–40.

This is a one-time, per-domain capital cost, after which the marginal cost of every future query is the cheap executor’s inference plus the injected skill tokens (skills average 45–143 markdown lines — small relative to typical agent contexts). The amortization logic:

break_even_queries ≈ C_evolve / (c_frontier_query − c_small_query)

Three structural observations, deliberately price-free (plug in current per-token rates when deciding — they change; the structure doesn’t):

  1. The gap being amortized is large. Per-token price ratios between frontier and small tiers are routinely an order of magnitude or more, and the substitution evidence (§6.2) is precisely that a small-tier model with skills does big-tier work on amenable tasks.
  2. Evolution cost scales with the task suite, not with the deployment volume. A few hundred to ~1,000 rollouts per evolution run on 16–80 training tasks is the entire discovery bill; production volume never re-pays it. Contrast fine-tuning: comparable one-time cost, but the artifact is locked to one base model and invisible to review.
  3. Transfer changes who pays for discovery. Because skills transfer across models (§4.4), the discovery bill can be paid by whichever model discovers best, once, and harvested by every executor that clears the capability floor — including future model generations, if the skills encode general procedures rather than workarounds.
Honest accounting

The paper never runs the exact configuration the thesis dreams of — frontier proposer + cheap executor throughout evolution. Its cross-model results transplant finished skills; the maintainer/proposer during any given evolution run is the same model as the executor. The closest supporting cells (e.g. Flash-evolved skills lifting Qwen-9B SpreadSheet 24.3→48.8, or Qwen-27B-evolved skills lifting 9B ALFWorld to 70.2) are strong but post-hoc transplants. Running the mixed configuration natively — strong optimizer, cheap executor, executor-in-the-loop rollouts — is an open experiment, and a cheap one. That is exactly what the MVP in §8 is designed to test.

7.1 Where this report sits#

Per research/README.md, this layer is informative, never normative. If anything here is ever to change the playbook, the path is fixed: finding → adjudication row in docs/playbook-development/EVIDENCE_MAP.md (with project corroboration and an A–H class) → chapter rule with evidence-strength label → playbook/CHANGELOG.md entry, with external sources recorded in playbook/references/sources.yaml. Two consequences worth stating now:

  • The promotion path wants both halves: an adjudication row pairs project evidence with external corroboration. WikiSkill supplies only the external corroboration half — and a single 3-day-old preprint is not yet “external consensus,” so on its own it supports at most a candidate default. The project-evidence half must come from an executed experiment such as §8.
  • The three primary sources here are sources.yaml-compatible as type: "paper" / "official-repo" entries (EXT-prefixed IDs) with evidence_strength: "research-only" until the lab reproduces anything — the honest label for a single 3-day-old arXiv preprint, however well-constructed.

7.2 What the playbook already believes, and where the gap is#

The striking thing about the survey is how much of the WikiSkill worldview the lab already practices — by hand:

  • The intervention ladder already predicts this result. Chapter 07 orders interventions so that evidence/retrieval/context (rung 2) and specification & verification (rung 4) precede fine-tuning (rung 7) and a larger model (rung 8). WikiSkill is a controlled, five-model demonstration of exactly that ordering: a rung-2/4 artifact (an evolved skill) recovering — and in places exceeding — the value of rung 8 (a 3× larger model) [P §4.2.1]. This is the single cleanest external corroboration the ladder has yet had in the skills direction.
  • Compiled context is already lab practice — one-shot, human-paced. The wholesaling HANDOFF_PROTOCOL.md §0 rule — “The lab session (which holds the full context) COMPILES context into the brief; the agent never follows links to acquire context” — is the report’s thesis in production: an expensive, context-rich session spends tokens once so a cheaper, context-poor session can execute. What it lacks relative to WikiSkill is the loop: briefs are compiled forward from human understanding, never evolved backward from measured execution traces, and nothing consolidates what the executing sessions learned.
  • Experience compilation exists but targets humans and evals, not agents. Chapter 12’s production-failure harvesting compiles failures into eval corpora; the PreCompact hook flushes session knowledge into durable docs; PLAYBOOK_FEEDBACK.md ledgers (F-1…F-11) accumulate candidate lessons. All of it is the Wiki Maintainer’s job description — performed by humans and lab sessions, consumed by humans, with no agent-facing pattern catalog and no optimizer reading it.
  • Skills are already named as experimental objects in projects/millwork-estimating/ (“Skills/workflows are experimental objects”; its pipeline hypothesis separately names “bounded agent skills”; WORKFLOW_HYPOTHESIS.md line 179 proposes “generic prompt vs reusable estimator skill” as a comparison) — a hypothesis waiting for exactly the harness this report describes.
  • The measurement discipline needed is already built. FIS runs frozen experiment contracts, hash-chained provenance, a test-look ledger, and stdlib statistics with a fixed verdict vocabulary — and its model gateway already runs Claude Code with --safe-mode to strip CLAUDE.md/skills/hooks, which is precisely the isolation WikiSkill’s methodology requires (measure the skill as the only moving part).
  • Verified absent: no playbook chapter, glossary term, or template covers packaged agent skills, persistent agent wikis, or expensive-model-compiles-for-cheap-model workflows. The gap is real, and it is doctrine-shaped, not tooling-shaped.

7.3 What integration could look like (three rungs of ambition)#

  1. Vocabulary and doctrine (cheap, low-risk). Glossary entries for agent skill, skill evolution, experience compilation; a chapter-08 (or 07) subsection placing evolved skills on the ladder as a rung-2/4 intervention with a stated capability floor and negative-transfer failure mode (§6.2), initially labeled research-only/doctrine — not yet exercised.
  2. A lab-operations wiki layer (medium). Extend the existing PreCompact flush into a maintained wiki/-style pattern catalog compiled from process logs and traces — with index.md descriptions written to the paper’s standard (problem + root cause + fix in one line [P App. E.2]) so future agent sessions, not just humans, can consume accumulated lab experience. This is valuable even if the full loop never ships, and it is the gist’s original use case [G].
  3. The closed loop (only after validation). A WikiSkill-style evolution rig — with the lab’s gate, not the paper’s: pre-registered thresholds, paired statistics, and a human freeze on skill adoption even where skill proposal is automated. The playbook’s governance doctrine and the paper’s architecture are compatible: gating-and-rollback is already a frozen-contract shape; skill-impact.md is already a CHANGELOG shape. But the lab should not adopt machinery it has not exercised — hence §8.

8A karpathy-scale MVP: skill evolution on a five-dollar-a-run harness#

The lab’s current test cases are big by design — FIS is a 312-file platform, wholesaling a 46 MB live-system intake. autoresearch demonstrates the opposite validation style: shrink everything except the loop until one experiment costs minutes and the overnight budget buys ~100 of them [A]. That is the right first exposure to skill evolution: the claim to validate is about the loop, not about any production domain.

8.2 The question worth answering first#

Not “does skill evolution work?” — the paper answered that across five models. The cell the paper left empty (§6.3) is the one this lab actually cares about:

Primary hypothesis

H1: With a cheap executor in the rollout loop and a frontier maintainer/proposer, evolved skills lift the cheap executor’s held-out accuracy significantly over (a) its no-skill baseline, and — secondarily — (b) skills evolved entirely by the cheap model, at comparable evolution cost. Concretely: executor Claude Haiku 4.5, optimizer Claude Opus 5 (per current first-party rates a 5× per-token spread — $1/$5 vs $5/$25 per Mtok in/out[9]), so discovery intelligence is bought where WikiSkill showed it matters (root-cause analysis, [P §5.1]) while rollout volume stays at the cheap tier — and the executor-in-the-loop design guards against the workaround-encoding failure that sank 4B→Flash transfer [P §4.2.2].

Three arms, one task suite: A no skill · B self-evolved (Haiku throughout) · C mixed (Haiku executes, Opus consolidates/proposes). A beats nothing, B replicates the paper’s direction of effect at the small tier, C tests H1. Each arm reports accuracy and cost-per-completed-task, because the economic claim is a ratio, not a score.

8.3 Task suite#

Requirements, distilled from the amenability findings [P §4.2.1]: deterministic scorer; tool-mediated, procedural work with recoverable errors; cheap-executor baseline in the 20–50% band so there is headroom in both directions; ~150 tasks total. Two candidates, in recommended order:

  1. Phase A — calibration on public ground: a drawn subset of SpreadsheetBench-style tasks (spreadsheet manipulation via code + shell was the single most skill-amenable domain measured: +6.5 to +40.9 across scales [P §4.2.1]), scored by the benchmark’s own checkers. Purpose: verify our rig reproduces the paper’s direction of effect before trusting it on anything else. Known risk: benchmark contamination in executor training data — acceptable here because the comparison is paired (skill vs no-skill on the same model), not absolute.
  2. Phase B — same rig, lab domain: millwork document microtasks (room-by-room coverage checks, finish-schedule reconciliation, cross-sheet reference following — the skill candidates PLAYBOOK_PATH.md §4 already names — against a surrogate document pack with hand-built answer keys). This converts WORKFLOW_HYPOTHESIS.md’s “generic prompt vs reusable estimator skill” from hypothesis to executed contract, and produces the project-evidence half of a future EVIDENCE_MAP row (§7.1).

rollouts  = K·(N_train + N_val) + N_val + 2·N_test        per arm-run
          = 8·(30+15) + 15 + 200  ≈ 575 executor trajectories

Illustrative, at ~5K in / 1K out per executor trajectory (Haiku 4.5)

Order autoresearch taught us to respect: this is an overnight-scale experiment. If a design iteration is wrong, the loss is a few dollars, not a milestone — this is what makes it safe to keep improving the rig until it earns trust.

8.5 Pre-registerable outcomes#

  • CONFIRMED: arm C beats arm A on held-out TEST with the pre-registered paired statistic at p < 0.05 (mirroring the paper’s bar [P App. C]) and a pre-registered minimum effect (suggest ≥5 points, below which the machinery isn’t worth its complexity). Secondary readout: C vs B on accuracy and total evolution cost.
  • REFUTED: C fails to beat A, or gains vanish on TEST (val-only gains = the seed-change pathology).
  • INCONCLUSIVE: effects positive but under the minimum, or seed variance swamps them — informative either way about domain amenability at the cheap tier. (The lab vocabulary’s fourth verdict, RANKED, fits the secondary three-arm ordering.)
  • Guardrails: wiki and skills inspected qualitatively after each run (are these general procedures or Haiku crutches? [P §4.2.2]); no mid-run threshold edits; every TEST evaluation logged against a look ledger.

Deliverables: the rig (a few hundred lines against three directories, in the spirit of autoresearch’s three files), the frozen contract, the run artifacts, and a dated follow-up report whose findings — not this report’s — would be the thing eligible for EVIDENCE_MAP adjudication.

9The meta possibility#

Flagged as speculation, because the recursion is real but untested at every level: the playbook’s own development process is already shaped like a WikiSkill loop running at human cadence. Project sessions generate experience (process logs, walktest findings, feedback ledgers F-1…F-11 — raw layer); maintainers consolidate it into EVIDENCE_MAP rows and research reports (wiki layer); promotions turn consolidated knowledge into chapter rules and templates (skills layer); the CHANGELOG’s what/why/evidence triple is skill-impact.md for humans. Under this mapping, a validated skill-evolution rig would not be a new capability bolted onto the playbook — it would be a faster inner loop for the process the playbook already runs on itself, with the same gates. The sensible order remains: validate the loop on a five-dollar harness (§8), use it inside one project, and only then ask whether the playbook’s own maintenance wants it. The paper’s ablation gives the warning label for skipping steps: knowledge layers pay off only when their boundaries are enforced (§4.5), and the playbook’s boundary — humans gate what becomes normative — is a feature, not a bottleneck to automate away.

10Open questions before any integration#

  1. Skill retrieval at scale. The paper injects all skills into the prompt and says plainly this dodges the triggering problem [P Limitations]. A real library needs progressive disclosure, and mis-triggering becomes a new failure class the playbook would have to instrument.
  2. Wiki pruning and drift. No garbage collection exists in the paper; the gist’s answer is a periodic lint pass [G]. Who prunes, on what evidence, without destroying the audit trail?
  3. Skills vs compiled briefs. The wholesaling protocol compiles per-session, disposable context; WikiSkill evolves durable, reusable procedures. When is each right? Plausible answer — briefs for one-off work, skills for recurring task families — but that boundary deserves its own note once both exist.
  4. Where the human gate sits. The paper auto-accepts on a validation statistic; the lab’s doctrine would keep humans on adoption. Does the loop still compound at human cadence, or does the value depend on tight iteration?
  5. Generalization across model generations. Skills that encode general procedures should survive model upgrades (the transfer results are encouraging [P Tab. 2]); skills that encode workarounds become silent tech debt on upgrade day. A re-validation trigger on model change belongs in any adopted doctrine.
  6. Where this fails. SealQA/OfficeQA-shaped work gained least [P §4.2.1]; knowledge-heavy, low-procedure domains may never repay evolution cost. The playbook’s domain-amenability question should be answered before a project buys the loop.
